Work

As an observing assistant, our main duty is to control the telescope and related equipment during the observing period and to provide support to astronomers by making optimum use of the facility. This entails finalizing an observing plan with the astronomer at the start of the night, properly focusing the secondary mirror and guider cameras, slewing the telescope to and priming the rotator for the target, monitoring telescope subsystems during exposures, and taking proper action when inclement weather is present.

Observing assistants serve as the person in charge of the safety of the telescope and facility during night-time observing, holding responsibility for terminating observing if conditions warrant ensuring that persons and equipment are not placed in jeopardy. Because the summit of Maunakea is very remote and at an altitude of roughly 14,000', observing assistants are required to asses extra steps when ensuring the saftey of night-time personnel such as monitoring for signs of high-altitude sickness and driving on icy road conditions.

Keck Observatory operates two identical 10 meter telescope and a total of 10 instruments. Observing assistants are required to be familiar with each instrument and how they change operating procedures. Keck's Adaptive Optics instruments (Osiris, NIRC2), both Natural Guide Star (NGS) and Laser Guide Star (LGS) modes, can be especially challengning and often require quick troubleshooting when issues arise to maximize time on-sky.

Research

I'm interested in how galaxies form their stellar disks. It's widely accepted galaxies form their stellar disks inside dark-matter halos that pull in surrounding hydrogen and helium gas clouds. As the gas cloud falls inward, it doesn't do so in a straight line, instead being twisted and pulled from other gas cloud clumps, imparting a spin. As the gas cloud collapses, it begins to heat up from collisions between gas particles, then eventually cools. This cooling causes the gas to lose its pressure support, falling inward more rapidly. However, its spin cannot dissipate like its temperature, and conservation of angular momentum requires the gas to begin spinning more rapidly as it falls inward and splay out into a disk. Once this disk is flattened and settled, it become remarkably dense, causing clumps of gas to begin bumping into each other and begin fragmenting into smaller clouds. These clouds undergo a similar collapse, heating their cores until it they are hot and dense enough to ignite nuclear fusion, birthing the first generation of stars in the disk.

There exists a rare breed of galaxies that house two co-spatial, co-planar stellar disks in the same galaxy that rotate in compete opposite directions. These galaxies are called Counter-Rotating Disk (CRD) galaxies. CRDs are one of the few astronomical objects in which a significant fraction of its stellar population formed through a different angular momentum event than the rest. This allows us to directly probe the specifics of stellar disk assembly in an already-mature galaxy - when did the secondary disk form? How chemically distinct is it from the pre-existing disk? How much stellar was was created in the event? How dynamically different was the new assembly event versus the original disks? How much opposite-directed mass and angular momentum can a mature stellar disk incorporate while preserving both its original structure and a coherent secondary disk?

The sample of CRDs has grown tremendously with the rise of Integral Field Spectroscopy, allowing galaxies to be described by spatially-resolved maps. From these maps, the CRD signature becomes very obvious - two sets of red-shifted and blue-shifted spaxels on either side of the galactic center in the stellar velocity map - two peaks on either side of the galactic center in the stellar velocity dispersion map, creating the classic "2σ" pattern.

CREDIT: Patrick Morrissey

With this growing sample, we can begin to answer the above questions. To do so requires extensive stellar population modeling and data calibration with high resolution observational data. Currently, I am working to build a reduction and analysis pipeline that takes raw Keck/KCWI data and generates the data products that directly answer these questions. The pipeline makes use of many open-source software packages - KSkyWizard, KcwiKit, KCWIDRP, pPXF, Pipe3D, and XookSuut.

At its core, CRD_DAP applies a pPXF fit to the CRD/KCWI spectra. pPXF parameterizes a second-order Gauss-Hermite polynomial and iteratively convolves a large library of stellar population spectra with this polynomial to create a Line-of-Sight Velocity Distribution (LOSVD) that best described the observed spectrum. Because CRDs have two stellar disks rotating in opposite directions, we expect one contribution of light to be slightly red-shifted and the other to be slightly blue-shifted. Therefore, modeling a single gaussian over two distinctly-separated LOSVDs will create a broad, ill-fitting model. Instead, CRD_DAP applies a two-component pPXF fit, allowing for both disks to be adequately modeled.

This two-component fit is one of the more involved pPXF calculations one can do. Stellar template degeneracy, component swapping, poor initial starting guesses, instrumental LOSVD broadening, and low S/N spectra all build upon one-another to muddle this calculation. Therefore, it's important to use one of the biggest telescope in the world to get the highest data quality possible, and to be extremely careful in the analysis procedure. Keck/KCWI handles the data quality, and CRD_DAP handles the careful analysis.

Instrumentation

I am very interested in instrumentation - specifically, building the instruments that directly enable doing astronomy. Recently, I have been working to design, build, align, test, and deploy a Reflectometer for the Keck Observatory. The goal of the reflectometer is to measure the reflectance of our primary, secondary, and tertiary mirrors before and after they get recoated to better characterize the decay of the reflective coating over time.

The general build layout is as follows: we must collimate light from a 0.22 numerical aperture light source ranging from 350nm - 1100nm; have at least a 1 meter separation between the collimating lens, the mirror surface, and the receiving lens; pass the light through an achromat doublet to limit chromatic aberration; have no more than a 4mm spot size into our integration sphere to collect the light; and ensure our configuration works for aspheric mirror surfaces and can survive the hard environment of Maunakea's summit.

1 / 6

To achieve this, I have built many Zemax model setups comparing different glass materials, achromat crown-flint combinations, lens curvature values, focal lengths, lens placement locations, and lens conic constants. Of these combinations, we compared the chromatic aberration, geometric spot size at the image plane, and the peak-to-valley wavefront error at the image plane as a function of the number of lenses in the system and the total transmission of the system. With the top 2 designs, I then ran Zemax's tolerancing wizard to determine the manufacturing requirements to build the custom lenses with varying constraints for lens radius, thickness, abbe number, refractive index, and irregularity. This allowed us to balance which setup would perform the best and as which is the most cost-effective. I then made ISO 10110 Standard drawings for each lens in the top 2 setups. We eventually went with two 75mm OD, 90% CA UV Fused Silica Plano-convex lenses from Thorlabs to act as our collimating and receiving lens.
